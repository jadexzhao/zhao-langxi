Research note · product thesis · still drafting

Can an AI concierge make room for better human choices?

What if your AI could understand your goals, communication style, and boundaries, then talk to other people’s AI systems before you ever had to scroll, explain yourself, or make small talk with hundreds of strangers? I do not have the answer yet. I have a product thesis, a research habit, and a responsibility to keep testing both.

Jade Zhao · rising senior · work in progress · last updated October 2026

What this note does not claim

  • Not a controlled experiment, pilot result, or personal conversion metric.
  • Not clinical, health-system, or internal lab framing.
  • Not a claim that every small business should buy a Digital Human.

What is still a question

Trust is easy to spend and hard to earn back. Disclosure belongs in the product, not in a footnote. Engagement should not mean keeping someone scrolling. It should mean reducing the exhausting parts of connection and returning a few choices that feel worth a person’s time. The system has to make room for both people.

This page records the work before a launch claim exists. I am interested in the day after the announcement, when the team has to answer the next question, fix the wrong one, and keep showing up. Say the true thing. Then revise when you were wrong.

My stance

I believe a Digital Human should give people more clarity, access, or control than the service path it replaces. It can make a useful answer easier to reach, especially when a small organisation is stretched. It cannot earn trust by pretending to be a person.

So the standard is demanding: disclose the system early, keep the person in control, make answers checkable, protect the human handoff, and give the organisation a way to maintain the experience. Repeat use should come from service that works, not from simulated intimacy or a face designed to make the system seem more human than it is.

A future AI concierge

The version of this idea I want to study is not a more persuasive chatbot. It is a private AI concierge that learns a person’s goals, insecurities, communication style, and limits with permission, then handles the exhausting first pass with other AI systems. In a dating context, those systems might talk to one another before two people ever meet. The output should be a short, explainable set of human possibilities, not an endless feed and not an automated decision about who someone is.

That concept raises the real product questions: can a person inspect and correct the profile being used, can they delete it, can they say no at every stage, and does the system reduce bad experiences without reducing human choice? I would only support the idea if the AI filters friction while people keep consent, context, and the final decision.

A hypothesis about Digital Humans

My reading of the public idea is that a Digital Human could become the interface for this concierge: not the person you are trying to date, and not a replacement for the relationship, but a warmer and more legible way to share context with the system. That is a hypothesis about the product direction, not a claim about Whitney Wolfe Herd’s private views or a prediction about what any company will ship.

1. A safer place to start

People may find it easier to say something vulnerable to a responsive face and voice than to a blank text box. That could help a concierge understand a recent breakup, a boundary, or a communication preference. It could also make disclosure feel more persuasive than it deserves to be. A warm expression is not evidence of understanding, so the system must say what it remembers and why it is asking.

2. A boundary around the human connection

The Digital Human could absorb repetitive introductions, awkward first passes, and some rejection before the person enters the real interaction. The goal would be to protect time and attention so people meet sooner when there is a reason to meet. The boundary matters: the avatar can filter friction, but it cannot grant consent, define compatibility, or speak for someone without their review.

3. A new interface norm

Swiping through a grid was once the new interface. Conversing with a Digital Human representative could be another shift, from selecting profiles to explaining what matters and receiving a small set of reasons to continue. The product earns that shift only if it gives people more agency than the feed did. Otherwise it is the same exhaustion wearing a friendlier face.

What the AI should learn, and what it should never become

The useful version of the onboarding conversation is not a generic personality quiz. With permission, an LLM could ask what a person learned from a breakup, what they do not want to repeat, and what kind of relationship they are trying to build. Attachment theory and other relationship research could help organize those signals, but the model should present them as working context, not as a diagnosis or a permanent label.

Voice standard

Not: “You have an anxious attachment style. Here are three profiles.” Better: “It sounds like feeling secure and chosen early on matters to you. Would you like to look for people who value clarity and steady communication?” The system should reflect what it heard, ask before interpreting, and leave the person room to disagree.

Accessibility is emotional equity

A system cannot ask someone to share vulnerable context while making the interface exhausting to read, hear, navigate, or correct. Accessibility is part of psychological safety: the person should be able to slow the conversation down, use text instead of voice, follow updates with a screen reader, and leave without losing their work.

  • Every voice interaction has an equivalent text path, with captions and visible status labels.
  • Every changing assistant message uses polite announcements instead of interrupting the person.
  • Every control works with a keyboard, remains visible at high zoom, and has a clear correction or reset path.
  • Every calming color still meets contrast requirements, with a user-controlled high-contrast mode.

There is also a hard boundary. The system should help people meet people, not become a virtual boyfriend or girlfriend, and not ask a user to fall in love with the product instead. A Digital Human may make the coaching conversation more approachable, but it must remain a door back to human life.

A three-step concierge workflow

  1. Start with the truth you choose to share

    The first interaction is a private conversation, not a generic bio form. Someone can describe a recent breakup, a boundary, or a communication pattern in their own words. The system may reflect and ask a useful next question, but it must not diagnose, pressure, or quietly keep what the person did not agree to store.

  2. Look for patterns without pretending certainty

    With permission, the model can organize stated preferences and observable context such as outdoor interests, routines, or communication needs. It should show what it inferred, let the person correct it, and distinguish a preference from a permanent identity.

  3. Hand back a few human possibilities

    Authorized assistants may compare boundaries and values in the background, but the output should be a small, explainable set of people worth a human conversation. The AI does not message strangers, impersonate anyone, or turn a recommendation into a commitment.

Non-negotiables

No catfishing. No deepfakes. No AI ghostwriting to real people. No virtual partner replacing the human relationship. The assistant can coach, organize, and filter friction; people keep identity, consent, and the final conversation.

Authenticity is a product requirement

The AI should help someone present their genuine strengths, not manufacture a different body, invent a life, or ghostwrite a relationship. A clearer photo, a more honest profile, and better self-understanding are coaching. Falsified media and automated messages are deception.

Compatibility should not require isolation

Better machine learning could understand specific lifestyle preferences inside one broad community instead of forcing every identity or interest into a separate silo. That promise only matters if people can see what shaped a recommendation and refuse categories that do not fit them.

Why the idea catches fire

Public coverage of this concept frames it as a response to dating burnout: too many conversations, too much repetition, and too little confidence that another swipe will lead to a good human interaction. The memorable version is a city-sized first pass that returns three people worth meeting instead of asking one person to perform six hundred conversations. That is a useful product provocation, not a measured result.

The same coverage also explains the discomfort. People do not want a system to turn intimacy into an opaque score, rehearse their personality without permission, or decide that an efficient match is the same thing as a meaningful one. The laughter and backlash are part of the research surface. They ask whether the product is removing exhaustion or removing the human part people came for.

Coverage, not proof

These articles document the public conversation around the AI-concierge idea. They do not establish that the system works, that users want it, or that any matching result is accurate. The closest cultural reference is the Black Mirror episode “Hang the DJ,” where simulated relationships are tested at scale before real people meet. It is a useful warning about optimisation becoming destiny, not a product specification.

What I am building toward

I am working toward a private AI concierge, not an avatar demo and not an automated dating decision. The concierge should listen with permission, represent a person’s boundaries, speak to other systems during the first pass, and return a short, explainable set of human possibilities.

A Friday night does not care how polished the interface looks if the answer is wrong, the memory cannot be corrected, or nobody knows what the system did. I grew up in a family Chinese restaurant in central Indiana. That is where the instinct started: make the next interaction easier without pretending the work disappeared.

Useful without taking over

The jobs that matter are narrow, inspectable, and keepable:

  • A private profile the person can inspect, correct, export, or delete.
  • A first pass that filters repetition without hiding uncertainty or conflict.
  • A short list of choices with reasons, not a mysterious ranking.
  • A clean path back to a human when the system should stop talking.

None of that requires pretending the system is a person or pretending the match is guaranteed. It requires accuracy, disclosure, consent at every meaningful step, and a handoff that still works when the student team leaves.
