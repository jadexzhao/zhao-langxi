Research note · product question · still drafting

Can a Digital Human earn a useful place?

When an AI system looks and communicates like a person, what makes someone want to use it, trust it, and come back ... especially when the organisation is small and the Tuesday is ordinary? I do not have the answer yet. I have a product question, a research habit, and a responsibility to keep testing both.

Jade Zhao · rising senior · work in progress · last updated October 2026

What this note does not claim

  • Not a controlled experiment, pilot result, or personal conversion metric.
  • Not clinical, health-system, or internal lab framing.
  • Not a claim that every small business should buy a Digital Human.

What is still a question

Trust is easy to spend and hard to earn back. Disclosure belongs in the product, not in a footnote. Engagement is not just getting the first question. It is helping the guest who needs a human and the staff member who has to maintain the answers. The system has to make room for both people.

This page records the work before a launch claim exists. I am interested in the day after the announcement, when the team has to answer the next question, fix the wrong one, and keep showing up. Say the true thing. Then revise when you were wrong.

My stance

I believe a Digital Human should give people more clarity, access, or control than the service path it replaces. It can make a useful answer easier to reach, especially when a small organisation is stretched. It cannot earn trust by pretending to be a person.

So the standard is demanding: disclose the system early, keep the person in control, make answers checkable, protect the human handoff, and give the organisation a way to maintain the experience. Repeat use should come from service that works, not from simulated intimacy or a face designed to make the system seem more human than it is.

What I am building toward

I am working on Digital Humans as useful business interfaces, not avatar demos. Looking human is not the product. The product is a place in a service path that still helps after the reel ends: accurate answers, clear disclosure, and a human handoff that does not make the guest repeat themselves.

For a restaurant, shop, or desk without a developer on call, the same rule shows up on the floor. A Friday night does not care how polished the face looks if the answer is wrong or nobody knows they are talking to a system. I grew up in a family Chinese restaurant in central Indiana. That is where the instinct started.

Useful for a small organisation

The jobs that matter are narrow and keepable:

  • After-hours answers that stay on published hours, menus, and policies.
  • Repeat questions that burn staff time.
  • A clean path to a human when the persona should stop talking.

None of that requires pretending the system is a person. It requires accuracy, disclosure, a reason to return, and a handoff that still works when the student team leaves.
