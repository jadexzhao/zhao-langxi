Research in public · product in progress

Digital Humans, built for the day after the demo

Looking human is not the product. Respect is.

I am a rising senior studying how a Digital Human could earn a place in an ordinary service path, especially for a small organisation that cannot afford a face that fails on the floor. The interesting part is not the face. It is whether the answer is useful, the disclosure is clear, and the handoff respects the person who needs help.

I am not presenting a finished company or a finished conclusion. I am building the research habit, testing the product questions, and leaving the work open to correction. Launch day is not the finish line. It is when the hard part becomes visible.

My stance: a Digital Human is worth building only when it gives people more clarity, access, or control than the old service path did. It should never borrow a human face to hide uncertainty, remove choice, or make a person feel trapped in a conversation with a system.

The next question is more ambitious: could a private AI concierge learn what matters to you, do the exhausting first pass with other systems, and return only a few human possibilities worth your time? That future is interesting only if the person stays in charge of the boundaries, the data, and the final yes.

Origin

A real floor is a better teacher than a perfect pitch

I am Chinese American and grew up in central Indiana (Greenfield, Indiana) around a family restaurant. A system either survives contact with a real customer or it does not. That lesson follows me into Serve IT handoffs and Digital Humans research: build for the person standing there, not for the screenshot. The restaurant floor taught me respect before I had a research vocabulary for it, and it still makes me rewrite when the first draft is lazy.

How I work

A small standard for a consequential product

  • Start with the person Name the ordinary moment Who is asking, what do they need, and what would make the interaction worth returning to?
  • Make the system legible Disclosure belongs in the experience People should know what they are using, where an answer came from, and when a human can take over.
  • Design for the day after A handoff is part of the product The work is not finished when the page ships. Someone still has to update it, answer for it, and improve it.

More rooms I'm working in

Same instinct, different homework

One sharp rule keeps me honest: respect the person when a system looks human. Digital Humans is the center of gravity here. The shipped websites and classroom work are where I learn whether an idea can survive contact with people, constraints, and the handoff after I leave. Each room has to earn its place.

I publish limits before claims because engagement is not a vanity number. It is whether someone understands what they are using, gets a useful answer, and chooses to come back.
